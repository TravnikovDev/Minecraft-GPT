# Record the real bot, script the rest

Start with a hybrid pipeline: record every bot session on a local Fabric 26.1 server with the ServerReplay mod, frame and export 1080x1920 shots from those recordings in the Flashback replay mod, and assemble each episode in the Remotion + narration + captions + Buffer pipeline that already produces Rotville, with overlays driven by the bot's own logs. The evidence points this way for three reasons: the audience in this niche watches what the AI does (behaviour videos get 100K to 1.9M views where project updates on the same channel get 29K to 74K), real game footage is the only visual route with near-zero build cost, and replay recording lets the camera be chosen after the fact, so a failed framing never costs a retake. A Blender recreation is feasible but costs an estimated 3 to 6 working days for one 10-second clip and 4 to 8 weeks for a usable session replayer, and no creator was found doing it. Headless rendering works for 26.1 only from an unreleased build and looks like a debug viewer. An AI coding agent can run everything except one step: neither replay mod has a command-line export, so framing and the export click stay with a person until a prototype shows the agent can drive that window. The format the evidence supports is a 60 to 100 second narrated episode about one visible behaviour, opened on the result or the failure, with the follow ask spoken right after the payoff. The name "minecraft-gpt" conflicts with both Mojang's and OpenAI's published naming rules and needs replacing on the channel before launch. Nothing in this report was run on the developer's machine: version and policy facts were read from registries, source code and official pages on 26 November 2026, and every "it works" statement about the M1 Max is still a claim to test.

## Behaviour gets the views; a changelog gets none

The closest comparable is Emergent Garden, whose Mindcraft project is also an LLM on Mineflayer. On that one channel, with one audience, the behaviour experiments are the hits ("4 AIs Survive 10 Days in Minecraft" at **1.9M views**, "AI Plays Minecraft Forever (and dies)" at 659K) while the engineering videos trail far behind ("Vision and Vibe Coding | Mindcraft Update" at 74K, "Mindcraft Research Paper!" at 29K) ([Emergent Garden Videos tab](https://www.youtube.com/@EmergentGarden/videos)). The multi-million long-form videos elsewhere tell the same story: all are framed as what the AI did, such as Kolanii's 11.2M-view "An AI Joined My Minecraft World..." ([Kolanii](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g2T-2-mvQgk)). At the other end, devlogs titled with the project name and a feature list sit at 3 to 61 views ([example](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jLRVeSRc93M)) or 459 views ([example](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-UX2yOSZ57s)). These are public counts read on 26 November 2026 from a small hand-picked sample, not a study, but the gap is two orders of magnitude and consistent.

No established short vertical devlog series about an LLM Minecraft bot was found, so the format has to be borrowed from neighbours. The best-evidenced template is Inbound Shovel: one fixed series title, one concrete development problem per episode, **76 to 100 seconds**, one upload a week, and **156K to 1.4M views** per Short ([Inbound Shovel Shorts tab](https://www.youtube.com/@InboundShovel/shorts)). Two shorter shapes also work nearby: "AI learns to X" payoff clips of 40 to 49 seconds, up to 11.8M views ([AI Warehouse](https://www.youtube.com/@aiwarehouse/shorts)), and 13 to 20 second personality or fail moments at 100K to 650K ([Neuro-sama](https://www.youtube.com/@Neurosama/shorts)). Raw clips do not travel on their own: Emergent Garden's Minecraft Shorts, uploaded square with bare label titles, got 3K to 20K views despite 294K subscribers ([Shorts tab](https://www.youtube.com/@EmergentGarden/shorts)), and Code Bullet's 3.47M subscribers yield only 5K to 45K on cut-down Shorts ([Code Bullet Shorts tab](https://www.youtube.com/@CodeBullet/shorts)). Shorts are their own product, and packaging per video is the lever a new channel controls.

The structure that follows is plain. Open on the result or the failure, state the premise in on-screen text, show the behaviour, give the architecture as a single sentence of "why" in the middle, and present "what changed" as a before/after of behaviour, never as a list. Code on screen belongs for a few seconds as proof. This suits the project unusually well: its commit messages are already written as behaviours ("Burning with a water bucket: poured at its feet"; "A lit home: torches on the room's walls and in a grid round the shelter"), and the bot keeps a code-written diary of what it did each game day. Both are ready-made script material (first-hand observation of the repository, not from the research notes).

On length, the public studies contradict each other and none is official. One vendor analysis favours 16 to 30 seconds for Shorts; Buffer's 2.7M-Reel dataset gives 90 to 179 seconds the best reach against an account's own baseline (+7.4%) and anything at 180 seconds or more a 49.3% penalty ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/instagram-reels-length/)). The position to take is that length follows format: **60 to 100 seconds for the narrated "what changed" episode**, 13 to 30 seconds for a single funny bot moment, and never over three minutes.

On the call to action, the brief asks for videos that end with a subscribe or follow ask. The one sizeable dataset (a vendor study of 4,914 short videos, correlational, skewed to commentary and news) found a follow ask associated with 1.25x a creator's median views, an ask placed mid-video with 1.37x, and an ask at the very end with 0.96x ([The Content Labs](https://thecontentlabs.app/blog/call-to-action-data-study)). So keep the ask, but speak it right after the main payoff or as the final line while footage is still running, and tie it to a promise about the next episode. Do not add a separate outro card. On YouTube the only officially backed device is the Related Video link, called out in the Short itself ([Social Media Today, quoting YouTube](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/youtube-offers-tips-on-working-with-shorts-videos/825096/)). Claims about hook timing in seconds, loops and animated-caption retention gains come only from tool vendors with no published method; the developer's own "viewed vs swiped away" figure in YouTube Analytics is the measure to trust.

### Filming the real game: replay recording beats live capture

All three pieces have builds for 26.1 in the Modrinth API: **Flashback 0.43.6**, ReplayMod 2.6.28 and the server-side **ServerReplay 3.4.3+26.1** ([Flashback](https://api.modrinth.com/v2/project/flashback/version), [ReplayMod](https://api.modrinth.com/v2/project/replaymod/version), [ServerReplay](https://api.modrinth.com/v2/project/server-replay/version)). Mineflayer's tested versions end at 26.1, which fixes the ceiling ([mineflayer version.js](https://github.com/PrismarineJS/mineflayer/blob/master/lib/version.js)). ServerReplay records a named player with no client open, either by command or automatically on join by a name rule, and writes ReplayMod or Flashback format ([ServerReplay README](https://github.com/senseiwells/ServerReplay)). Flashback takes any even resolution, has a 9:16 aspect mode that pre-fills 1080x1920, and bundles FFmpeg natives for macOS arm64 ([StartExportWindow.java](https://github.com/Moulberry/Flashback/blob/master/src/main/java/com/moulberry/flashback/editor/ui/windows/StartExportWindow.java), [build.gradle](https://github.com/Moulberry/Flashback/blob/master/build.gradle)). Its keyframe types include Camera, Camera Orbit, Track Entity, Timelapse, Speed and Time of Day ([keyframe types](https://github.com/Moulberry/Flashback/tree/master/src/main/java/com/moulberry/flashback/keyframe/types)), and an add-on overlays the vertical frame while framing ([Flashback Extras](https://modrinth.com/mod/flashback-extras)). Rendering is offline, so quality does not depend on live frame rate.

That combination answers the brief's main demand. A third-person tracked or orbiting camera shows the bot as a character doing things, where `/spectate` gives only the bot's own first-person view with no body in frame ([Minecraft Wiki](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Commands/spectate)). Long builds compress into seconds with a Timelapse keyframe while the camera keeps moving at normal speed ([Flashback changelog](https://modrinth.com/mod/flashback/changelog)). Speeding the server up is not a substitute: `/tick rate` above the default does not speed up players, so it would not make the bot work faster ([Minecraft Wiki](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Commands/tick)).

Live capture is the fallback. A spectator account in a 9:16 window, recorded by OBS (native arm64 build) or by ffmpeg's `avfoundation` device, starts fastest and is fully scriptable, but framing is fixed at record time ([OBS](https://obsproject.com/download), [FFmpeg devices](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-devices.html#avfoundation)).

### Blender recreation: feasible, expensive, and nobody does it

The building blocks exist. **MCprep 3.6.3** (12 July 2026) states support for Blender 2.83 through 5.2, bundles player and mob rigs under CC-BY, exposes everything as ordinary operators, and runs them under `--background` in its own tests ([MCprep releases](https://github.com/Moo-Ack-Productions/MCprep/releases), [run_tests.py](https://github.com/Moo-Ack-Productions/MCprep/blob/master/run_tests.py)). The world is the weak link on a Mac. jmc2obj has a full command line, but its 26.1 world-format fixes exist only in unreleased commits from August 2026; the tagged release stops at 1.21.9 ([jmc2obj commits](https://github.com/jmc2obj/j-mc-2-obj/commits/master), [releases](https://github.com/jmc2obj/j-mc-2-obj/releases)). Mineways reads 26.3 and earlier but is a Windows program whose packaged Mac build is two major versions behind ([Mineways downloads](https://www.realtimerendering.com/erich/minecraft/public/mineways/downloads.html)). The one active schematic importer is Windows-only ([Mine2Blend](https://github.com/CAT-YC/Mine2Blend)). The version-proof route is a custom one: the bot dumps a box of blocks and a tick-rate timeline, and a Python script meshes it in Blender and drives a six-box character from the log.

The steelman is real. This route reuses the Rotville pipeline, gives total camera freedom and a consistent cartoon identity, and with original textures and an original robot character it keeps Mojang's assets off screen entirely. Three things count against starting here. First, cost: the researcher's estimate is **3 to 6 working days for one 10-second clip** and **4 to 8 weeks** for a replayer that handles walking, mining, placing and a few mobs by day, with stairs, crops, chests, combat and night scenes each adding more. Second, proof: a devlog about real behaviour is less convincing when it is not the game. Third, novelty: no creator and no agent project (Voyager, Mindcraft) was found presenting bot behaviour through an offline renderer, so there is no audience evidence for it, although that search was two web queries and is not proof of absence. Render cost is also unknown: no trustworthy seconds-per-frame figure for EEVEE on an M1 Max in Blender 5.x was found, and the Rotville render logs on the same machine predict it better than anything online.

### Code-driven and headless rendering: an overlay layer, not the main footage

prismarine-viewer was revived in autumn 2026 and its master branch lists 26.1, but the npm release (1.33.0, February 2025) and the README stop at 1.21.4 ([version.js](https://github.com/PrismarineJS/prismarine-viewer/blob/master/viewer/lib/version.js), [npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/prismarine-viewer)). Its headless mode pipes JPEG frames to ffmpeg at any size, but it is first-person only, a third-person request sits unanswered, and an unmerged fix documents that recordings play at the wrong speed and start on blank sky ([lib/headless.js](https://github.com/PrismarineJS/prismarine-viewer/blob/master/lib/headless.js), [issue #477](https://github.com/PrismarineJS/prismarine-viewer/issues/477), [PR #504](https://github.com/PrismarineJS/prismarine-viewer/pull/504)). Its native `gl` dependency has no prebuilt binary for Node 24, so it needs a separate Node 20 or 22 process beside the Bun project ([PR #522](https://github.com/PrismarineJS/prismarine-viewer/pull/522)). It is the only zero-GUI path and useful as an automated "bot's-eye" insert. The browser client mcraft.fun stops at server 1.21.5 ([minecraft-web-client](https://github.com/zardoy/minecraft-web-client)).

Motion graphics are where code-driven rendering earns its place, because they show how the bot decides. Task and priority labels, a diary line, an inventory strip, a reflex interrupt badge and a three-box architecture diagram are ordinary HTML in a Remotion composition. Remotion is current (4.0.547, 24 November 2026), free for an individual under its licence, and ships official agent skills for authoring, captions and rendering ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/remotion), [LICENSE.md](https://github.com/remotion-dev/remotion/blob/main/LICENSE.md), [Agent Skills](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/ai/skills)). Code animation is covered by the official Code Hike template ([template-code-hike](https://github.com/remotion-dev/template-code-hike)). A 3D block mini-replay inside Remotion is plausible through `@remotion/three`, which requires Chromium's ANGLE renderer ([@remotion/three docs](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/three)), but no source documents the Minecraft libraries (deepslate, skinview3d) running inside a frame-by-frame renderer, so that is a spike, not a plan. HeyGen's HyperFrames (Apache-2.0, v0.9.98) is the one credible alternative framework; with a working Remotion pipeline there is no reason to switch ([HyperFrames](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes)).

### How far an agent gets end to end

With shell access, recording is fully automatable today: start the server, start the bot, run a scenario through owner chat, let ServerReplay record by name, stop, collect the file. So are the script from the git range and diary, narration, captions, the Remotion render and scheduling. The gap is the export. ReplayMod's documentation describes no scripted rendering, and Flashback has an in-GUI export queue and a batch add-on but no command line ([ReplayMod docs](https://www.replaymod.com/docs/), [FlashBatch](https://modrinth.com/mod/flashbatch)). Two things narrow the gap. Flashback can pin export settings in config so the export window always opens at 1080x1920 with the right codec ([ForceDefaultExportSettings.java](https://github.com/Moulberry/Flashback/blob/master/src/main/java/com/moulberry/flashback/configuration/ForceDefaultExportSettings.java)), and it stores camera keyframes as JSON per replay ([EditorStateManager.java](https://github.com/Moulberry/Flashback/blob/master/src/main/java/com/moulberry/flashback/state/EditorStateManager.java)), so an agent could write camera tracks computed from the bot's logged positions. That JSON is undocumented and has broken between versions before. Desktop control of the Minecraft window is the third option and is untested. In Blender the agent's reach is the widest (the MCP server runs arbitrary `bpy`), which is the route's best argument and does not change its build cost.

## The pipeline to start with, and a first episode

The recommended stack has four layers, each chosen for the least new work.

**Capture.** Run the test server as Fabric 26.1 with Fabric API, Fabric Kotlin and ServerReplay, set to auto-record the bot's name in Flashback encoding. Keep client and server on exactly 26.1. Every session then leaves a replay file on disk with no client open, which becomes the footage archive for each batch of work.

**Flight recorder.** Add a small read-only recorder to the bot that writes a JSON-lines timeline: wall-clock and game time, position, the current task and its priority bucket, reflexes firing, and diary lines as they are written. The Blender note estimated half a day to a day for a comparable recorder, and the project's action primitives and diary hooks are the natural places to tap. This file is what turns footage into an explanation, and the same file would feed a Blender or three.js replay later, so the work is not wasted whichever way the visuals evolve.

**Shots.** Open the replay in Flashback on the Mac, set 9:16, place Track Entity or Orbit keyframes and a Timelapse pair, and export 1080x1920 H.264 clips with the game HUD hidden. Expect a few minutes of framing per clip once learned (an estimate from the capture research, not a measurement). Skip shaders at first: each rendering mod is another thing that breaks, and Flashback's own changelog records repeated Iris breakage ([Flashback changelog](https://modrinth.com/mod/flashback/changelog)).

**Assembly and publishing.** Feed the clips, the timeline and a script into a new composition in the existing Remotion project: footage full-frame, word-timed captions, the decision overlay, a few seconds of code, the spoken follow ask. Because narration is synthesised from a known script, forced alignment gives exact word times and avoids speech-recognition errors on names like "Mineflayer" ([ElevenLabs forced alignment](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/capabilities/forced-alignment)); Remotion's `createTikTokStyleCaptions()` pages the result ([docs](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/captions/create-tiktok-style-captions)). Publish one clean 1080x1920 master through Buffer, whose API covers YouTube, TikTok and Instagram ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/social-media-api-multi-platform-posting/)). Buffer matters most for TikTok, where a self-built API client can only post privately until TikTok audits it ([TikTok Direct Post reference](https://developers.tiktok.com/doc/content-posting-api-reference-direct-post)).

For the first episode, pick the behaviour with the clearest visible payoff from the latest batch. The suggestion below uses the "go live your life" switch and the night routine, both present in the repository; treat it as a proposal to adjust to what the recording shows.

| Time | Picture | Voice and text |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 3 s | Night, mobs outside a lit shelter, the bot safe inside | The result first, as on-screen text: it survived its first night and nobody told it how |
| 3 to 12 s | The owner's chat line; the bot walks off alone | The premise in one sentence |
| 12 to 35 s | Timelapse with an orbiting camera: wood, tools, shelter, torches, door | Overlay shows the current task and its priority; narration names each step |
| 35 to 50 s | One interruption (a creeper or a fall into water) | Overlay shows the reflex taking over while the task waits; one sentence on the design: a model plans the day, code moves the hands; three seconds of real code |
| 50 to 65 s | Before/after split of the behaviour this batch changed | What changed, shown as behaviour; the follow ask with a promise about the next episode |
| 65 to 75 s | Morning, the door opens | Closing line that loops back to the opening |

Plan the series, not the video. Vendor benchmarks put a new channel at 50 to 500 views per Short in the first 48 hours with the first 20 to 50 uploads behaving inconsistently ([Humble&Brag benchmarks](https://humbleandbrag.com/blog/youtube-shorts-benchmarks), [Humble&Brag new channels](https://humbleandbrag.com/blog/new-youtube-channel-average-views)); those numbers are low-confidence, but they argue for judging the concept after a batch of 20 to 30 episodes, at the weekly cadence that works for Inbound Shovel, with short bot-moment clips mixed in when a recording yields one.

## Rules to settle before the first upload

### The name conflicts with two rulebooks

Mojang's Usage Guidelines allow "Minecraft" only in a secondary name, subtitle or description, used to describe the work honestly, and forbid it as the primary or dominant part of a name or title; names confusingly similar to it count too, and no Mojang logo, font or texture may form part of the branding ([Minecraft Usage Guidelines](https://www.minecraft.net/en-us/usage-guidelines)). The guidelines' own examples accept a distinctive name followed by "a Minecraft ..." description and reject a title that begins with "Minecraft". OpenAI's brand guidelines separately do not permit the GPT brand in app, product, developer or company names, while allowing a precise reference to the model used ([OpenAI brand guidelines](https://openai.com/brand/)). **"minecraft-gpt" breaks both patterns at once.** The compliant shape is an own distinctive name, with "an AI bot for Minecraft, built with" the model named in the subtitle or description, and the disclaimer Mojang asks for in the channel and video descriptions: "NOT AN OFFICIAL MINECRAFT PRODUCT. NOT APPROVED BY OR ASSOCIATED WITH MOJANG OR MICROSOFT" ([Minecraft Usage Guidelines](https://www.minecraft.net/en-us/usage-guidelines)). The same guidelines apply the naming rule to websites and listings, so the repository and site names deserve the same review. This is a reading of published text, not legal advice; no enforcement cases against small channels were researched, and Mojang states it does not advise on individual projects.

### Footage is clearly permitted; recreated assets are tolerated, not granted

The guidelines permit sharing and ad-monetising videos of playing or using Minecraft, on four conditions that matter here: the video is free to view, the creator adds enough original content (own commentary qualifies, a logo or overlay alone does not), the video does not exist to advertise something unrelated to Minecraft, and sponsor notices sit outside the game footage ([Minecraft Usage Guidelines](https://www.minecraft.net/en-us/usage-guidelines)). Narrated footage of the developer's own bot fits squarely. A Blender scene built from Mojang's textures and models is different: it uses "assets" but is not footage, the word "animation" appears nowhere in the guidelines, and their catch-all says uncovered uses are probably unwanted. Fan animation made this way is openly tolerated in practice (MCprep bundles vanilla textures and asks users to own the game), but the permission is informal and revocable ([MCprep README](https://github.com/Moo-Ack-Productions/MCprep)). That is one more reason to film first, and to use original assets if Blender is added later.

### Platform rules that shape the file, the voice and the music

One **1080x1920 H.264 MP4 of up to three minutes** serves all three platforms. YouTube classifies vertical videos up to three minutes as Shorts, and a Short longer than one minute with any active Content ID claim is blocked worldwide ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15424877?hl=en)). Instagram warns that Reels over three minutes are not recommended to new audiences ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/instagram-reels-length/)). Both YouTube and Instagram tightened originality rules in 2026 against re-uploads of other people's content, and Instagram excludes watermarked video from recommendations; no rule found penalises a creator's own file on several platforms ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/youtube-updates-shorts-algorithm-to-put-more-focus-on-original-content/831976/), [Instagram Creators blog](https://creators.instagram.com/blog/rewarding-original-creators-on-instagram)). Safe-zone pixel boxes are vendor estimates that disagree; keep captions and action in the central region and preview in each app.

The Content ID rule makes music the main hazard of a 60 to 100 second episode. Mojang permits its approved tracks (the "Minecraft Creator-Safe" playlist) in monetised Minecraft videos with a credit ([minecraft.net](https://www.minecraft.net/en-us/article/can-i-use-minecraft-music-)), but whether those tracks raise Content ID claims was not researched. Until tested, use no music or a track that carries no claim.

On YouTube, gameplay footage, clearly animated content and a clone of the creator's own voice all sit outside the altered-content disclosure requirement ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)). The monetisation risk is different: the "inauthentic content" policy denies monetisation to content that looks template-made with minimal variation, while explicitly allowing a shared intro and outro and series whose episodes each have a distinct focus ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)). An automated pipeline is fine; near-identical episodes with a generic script are not. For TikTok and Instagram, the available summaries of the official pages say realistic AI audio needs the platform's AI label, so a synthetic-voice cut should carry it there (those pages could not be opened directly). Set the audience to "not made for kids" deliberately: the made-for-kids setting switches off comments, the notification bell and end screens, which a devlog depends on ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9527654?hl=en)).

Monetisation should not shape the format. The YouTube Partner Program needs 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 watch hours or 10M Shorts views in 90 days ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72851?hl=en)), and press coverage reports that doubling for new applicants from 1 February 2027 ([Digital Music News](https://www.digitalmusicnews.com/2026/08/10/youtube-doubles-eligibility-requirements-partner-program/)). One housekeeping item if the Rotville pipeline uses them: OpenAI lists `gpt-4o-mini-tts` and `tts-1` for shutdown on 6 January 2027 and `whisper-1` on 26 February 2027 ([OpenAI deprecations](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/deprecations)).

Five short prototypes remove most of the risk, in this order. The first is a half-day smoke test of the whole capture path: a Fabric 26.1 server with ServerReplay, one bot session, the file opened in Flashback 0.43.6, one tracked shot exported at 1080x1920. It answers whether the bot is recorded at all, whether Flashback opens ServerReplay's output without conversion, how long an export takes on the M1 Max, and whether the bot's skin shows on an offline-mode server. The second measures the human cost: frame three shots by hand with a timer, then let the agent try the same export through desktop control and through a templated keyframe file copied from a hand-made one. If neither works, a few minutes of manual framing per clip is an acceptable standing cost at one episode a week. The third is synchronisation: confirm that the flight recorder's timestamps line up with replay time closely enough for overlays to land on the right frame. The fourth is a private upload of a 70-second cut with the intended music to see whether a claim appears, plus a check that the file posts publicly to all three platforms through Buffer. The fifth, only if the cartoon look still appeals after two or three episodes, is the Blender spike scoped to one 10-second clip with original textures, timed for build effort and seconds per frame.

Two risks sit outside prototypes. Mojang is moving Java Edition from OpenGL to Vulkan, which will cost rendering mods more than a normal update on later versions ([minecraft.net](https://www.minecraft.net/en-us/article/another-step-towards-vibrant-visuals-for-java-edition), [GamingOnLinux](https://gamingonlinux.com/2026/02/minecraft-java-is-switching-from-opengl-to-vulkan-for-the-vibrant-visuals-update)); pinning the filming setup to 26.1 keeps the series clear of that for now. And Flashback is all-rights-reserved software by one author with no public documentation, so ReplayMod stays the named fallback for the same recordings.

## Conclusion

The four candidate routes turn out not to be rivals. The game supplies proof, code-driven graphics supply the explanation, the agent supplies the labour, and Blender is an optional later identity layer. The piece that connects them is the flight recorder: a timeline of what the bot did and why, written by the bot itself. With it, real footage gains an honest view into the decision layer, and any later renderer has its input waiting. Without it, every route can only show a character walking around.

The research also moved the bottleneck. Tooling is not the constraint, since every stage but one already runs from a shell. The constraints are a single unscriptable export window, an unproven audience for short-form bot devlogs, and a name that two brand owners' rules reject. The first is a half-day test, the second needs twenty to thirty episodes to judge, and the third is a decision to make before any of it is published.
